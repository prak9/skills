# Four-Quadrant Completeness Lens

Use this lens to widen a narrow frame before applying crux judgment. It helps answer two questions: which part of reality is missing, and which worldview is being mistaken for the whole truth?

Do not use the matrix as a decision algorithm. Scan all four quadrants, trace their interactions, then return to the one or two variables that can change the decision.

## Map the four realities

| | Interior: meaning and interpretation | Exterior: observable behavior and systems |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | values, motives, emotions, fears, identity, attention, lived experience | skills, habits, health, behavior, output, track record, measurable constraints |
| **Collective** | norms, relationships, shared meanings, family or team expectations, status, culture | incentives, institutions, markets, technology, policy, capital, infrastructure, platform rules |

The same event can appear in every quadrant. A career problem may involve a desire for autonomy, an untested skill gap, a family definition of status, and a shrinking labor market. None alone is the whole explanation.

## Run the scan

### 1. Fix the focal question

State the decision, actor, outcome, and time horizon. Do not scan “my life” or “the business” as a whole.

```text
Weak: Should I change careers?
Better: Should I spend the next six months testing a move from operations to product work without reducing household runway below nine months?
```

### 2. Fill each quadrant with evidence and uncertainty

Ask only the questions relevant to the focal decision.

**Individual interior**

- What does the person actually value, fear, desire, or avoid?
- Is the stated preference stable, or is it a reaction to fatigue, shame, conflict, or recent reward?
- Which identity story makes one option feel impossible or compulsory?

**Individual exterior**

- What can the person demonstrably do?
- What do behavior, health, habits, work samples, and repeated outcomes show?
- Which capability or resource can be tested through a small real action?

**Collective interior**

- Which family, team, peer, professional, or social norms define success here?
- Whose approval, trust, language, or status rules shape the choice?
- Which “personal preference” may actually be a borrowed expectation?

**Collective exterior**

- Which incentives, institutions, market structure, technology, policy, and platform rules constrain the outcome?
- What does the system reward, punish, subsidize, or make invisible?
- Which external trend is real, and which is only a popular narrative?

Mark every entry as `fact`, `interpretation`, or `unknown`. Do not let a compelling inner story outrank external evidence by default, or let a measurable market variable erase lived experience.

### 3. Trace cross-quadrant loops

Look for causality across boxes instead of treating them as independent lists.

```text
platform incentive -> team norm -> individual habit -> measured output -> stronger platform incentive
```

Name reinforcing loops, balancing loops, delays, and who bears the cost. An intervention in one quadrant can fail because another quadrant restores the old behavior.

### 4. Rotate worldview lenses

Interpret the same evidence through several legitimate value systems:

- **Tradition/order lens:** continuity, authority, duty, legitimacy, stability.
- **Modern/performance lens:** evidence, efficiency, competition, scale, measurable progress.
- **Pluralist/context lens:** voice, equity, inclusion, power, local meaning, unintended exclusion.

For each lens, ask what it notices, what it optimizes, what it treats as a cost, and what it tends to miss. Use the lenses as tools, not ranks of human development. A later or more fashionable lens is not automatically better; a single decision may require constraints from all three.

Do not manufacture false balance. Evidence can still make one interpretation wrong, and rights or safety can rule out an efficient option.

### 5. Compress to the crux

After the scan:

1. Identify conflicts between quadrants and lenses.
2. Rank variables by impact × uncertainty.
3. Ask which fact, if reversed, would flip the decision.
4. Name one or two crux variables and explain why the remaining factors are secondary, fixed, or downstream.
5. Choose the cheapest observation or reversible action that can resolve the crux.

Completeness earns the right to simplify; it does not forbid simplification.

## Deliver the analysis

Use the lightest form that preserves the reasoning:

```text
Focal decision and time horizon:
Quadrant map: facts | interpretations | unknowns
Cross-quadrant feedback loops:
Worldview lenses: contribution | blind spot
Main tension or tradeoff:
Crux variable(s):
Evidence that would change the judgment:
Smallest reversible next step:
```

Do not reproduce the full matrix when only one omitted quadrant matters. Lead the final answer with the crux, not with four equal sections, unless the user explicitly asks for a quadrant report.

## Avoid common failures

- Filling four boxes with synonyms and calling it completeness.
- Treating emotions as noise or treating introspection as proof.
- Explaining individual behavior only through character while ignoring incentives and constraints.
- Explaining every choice through systems while denying agency, skill, or responsibility.
- Treating culture as a stereotype instead of a testable shared pattern.
- Ranking people by worldview label or using “higher stage” to dismiss disagreement.
- Listing perspectives without resolving conflict, evidence, or action.
- Giving all quadrants equal weight after the decisive variable is known.
